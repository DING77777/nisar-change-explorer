Every case goes through the same steps: three key dates, and the change between the first two and between the last two. Values are medians over the whole area shown, in decibels (dB). Positive means brighter. A whole-area median mixes land that changed with land that did not, so open a case to see where the change is and what the labelled groups show.

How each case is made

  1. Take three NISAR L2 GCOV images from the same track and frame. We use the HH and HV backscatter layers (L-band, Frequency A).
  2. Keep only pixels that are valid on all dates in both polarisations, using the product's own quality layers.
  3. Average the radar power over a small window to reduce speckle, then convert to decibels.
  4. Subtract to get the change between the first two dates and between the last two. Where the first two dates are both before an event, the first change shows how much the place varies normally.
  5. Report both changes next to each other, with independent evidence where we have it: river gauges, or groups of cells labelled from optical images without looking at the radar.
  6. Where more dates exist, plot the median return on every date so the event can be compared with months of normal variation.

Users can highlight and export cells that pass a stated rule; these are candidates for checking, not a validated hazard classification, and we do not draw a flood boundary. The page shows where the surface changed, how that compares with a normal 12 days, and what else supports or limits the reading.

Why radar, and why NISAR

Radar works at night and through cloud, which is when optical satellites fail during storms. NISAR is the NASA-ISRO radar mission. Its L-band radar has a longer wavelength than the C-band radar of Sentinel-1, it images land every 12 days, and its data are free and open.

Which polarisation shows the change?

It depends on what changed. After heavy rain in Queensland, HH brightened more than HV across the whole landscape. At Sale Common, where land turned into open water, both dropped and HV dropped a little more (4.2 dB against 3.7 dB). In Burdekin field C, HV fell by about 11 dB and HH by about 8 dB. A handful of cases is not enough to say one polarisation is better in general, so the page always shows both.

Four kinds of change, one recipe

Water (Sale Common, Queensland), farmland (Burdekin), fire (Rockadooie) and a mountain hazard (Timure) all go through the same steps and the same page. Each case is one config file. Where a team member delivered values already computed per cell (Rockadooie) or radar already averaged to 100 m (Burdekin) or 50 m (Timure) in dB, the recipe reads them as they are and never converts dB a second time.

What NISAR could do next

  • NISAR images land every 12 days, so a place builds up a long record. A longer series would describe the normal rhythm of a place better than a few dates.
  • NISAR's interferometric products measure ground movement, which backscatter cannot. At Timure the products we could get failed quality checks at the source; better coverage may change that.
  • Testing the exported candidate cells against field checks, so a radar-only rule can be trusted where no optical labels exist.

These are directions for future work, not demonstrated on this page.

What we checked for the Queensland case

  • The before and after images were made with different processing versions. We measured the difference using one acquisition that exists in both versions, and compared four ways of correcting for it.
  • We compared the change with river gauge records, two rainfall datasets, land cover and government event records.
  • We looked for an official flood extent to test against and did not find one.
